Lunar Landing Site Finder

Tell us what your mission needs. We search the whole Moon, using NASA LOLA terrain and JPL Horizons Sun and Earth positions, and show the regions that meet your requirements.

Ice survives only where it stays below about −163 °C: permanently shadowed crater floors near the poles. We mark those from NASA LOLA terrain (does the Sun ever clear the rim?) and LRO Diviner temperatures, and rate every named polar crater. A lander needs sunlight and Earth contact, so it lands nearby and a rover drives in.

Daylight and Earth % are measured over your whole mission period, counting crater rims and mountains. Away from the poles the Sun is up about half of each month, so 50% daylight or less is realistic there; only polar peaks and rims do better. Average panel sunlight is the mean over the whole period, nights included (a flat panel facing up gets 1,361 W/m² at most).

        No air, no twilight

        The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no blue sky, no twilight and no light bending over the horizon. The instant the Sun drops behind a crater rim, a site goes from full sunlight to deep space cold. That is why this app traces the real terrain horizon (NASA LOLA) instead of a flat one: a mountain 50 km away can decide whether a lander has power.

        The polar Sun skims the horizon

        The Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5° to its path around the Sun. At the poles the Sun never climbs more than about 1.5° above a flat horizon: it circles the sky, sliding along the edge. Raise a site 100 m on a ridge and it may see the Sun for weeks; drop into a crater and it may never see it at all. Try the horizon panorama in the Site tab on a polar site.

        Earth hangs still, but wobbles

        The Moon always shows Earth the same face, so from any one spot Earth stays in nearly the same part of the sky. The near side always has Earth up; the far side never does. Near the edge (the limb), and at the poles, Earth sits right on the horizon and the Moon's wobble (libration, about 7°) lifts it into view or hides it behind terrain over days and weeks. No Earth in view means no direct radio contact without a relay satellite.

        Why ice hides in polar craters

        Water ice can survive for billions of years only where the surface stays below about −163 °C. At the poles, crater floors the Sun never reaches are among the coldest places measured in the Solar System (below −230 °C). The 2009 LCROSS impact into Cabeus crater threw up water. That is why missions go to the south pole, and why they have to land on the sunny rims nearby.

        Small changes, big effects

        Because the Sun and Earth sit so low at the poles, a few kilometres or a few days can change everything. Use Nearby alternatives (Site tab) to see sunlight and Earth contact for every point within 3 km, the −1 d / +1 d / +1 mo buttons to step the dates, and Best landing dates to find the next good launch slot.

        How to read this app

        Globe colours (after a search): light blue = meets everything with ice in range, green = meets everything, yellow = close. Stat tiles fill up by how good the value is. Hover any i for the reasoning. Data: JPL Horizons (Sun and Earth positions, including libration), NASA LOLA (terrain), LRO Diviner (temperatures).
